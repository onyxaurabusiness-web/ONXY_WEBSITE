What a Table Says

3 min read · Essay II of V

A table is a sentence written in objects.

Before anyone sits, it has already spoken. The linen says how the evening is expected to go. The glasses say how long it will last. The candles say whether anyone is in a hurry.

Water is rarely part of the sentence. It arrives late, in whatever vessel was nearest, and is poured before anyone has noticed it. It is the last thing chosen and the first thing served.

This is strange. Of everything on a table, water is the one thing common to every guest. It stays from the first course to the last. It is poured without being asked for. And yet it is often the one thing nobody chose.

Glass does something few other objects on a table can do: it holds the candlelight and returns it. A clear bottle is a small lantern. It takes the room’s light and gives some of it back, which is why the distance between a candle and a bottle is worth a moment’s thought.

A table does not need more. It needs fewer things, chosen.

Think of the last dinner you remember well. You probably cannot say what the water was. You may remember the light, a particular glass, the way the plates were cleared. A table is remembered as a whole, and the whole is made of small things that did not interrupt each other.

A good table is mostly subtraction. One bottle rather than three. One glass for each person, set where the hand will find it. Nothing that needs explaining. When a table is working, no one remarks on it. They notice, much later, that they were never once distracted.

There is a discipline in leaving things off. Every addition has to earn its place, because every addition is one more thing for the guest to read. A bottle with three lines of text invites three lines of attention. A bottle with a name, a mark and nothing else asks for a glance and then gets out of the way.

The best thing a room can say about its water is short. If the person pouring needs a paragraph to explain it, the guest will not listen to the paragraph. If they can say it in a sentence, the guest will remember the sentence, and will repeat it, quietly, to someone else.

Guests rarely remember the water. They remember that nothing was out of place. This is the whole difficulty and the whole ambition: to be present at every table and noticed at none, until the evening is over and someone asks, “What was that we were drinking?”

A bottle on a table is also a small act of respect. It says that the host thought about the last thing as carefully as the first.

When it works, a host never has to say any of this. The bottle is simply there, the glass is simply full, and the conversation goes on over it as if it had always been that way.

Water at a table should be the quietest thing on it, and the thing that would be missed if it were taken away.
